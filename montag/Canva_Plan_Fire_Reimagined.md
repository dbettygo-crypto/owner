# Canva Plan: "Fire Reimagined" (Option C)

**Format:** 2 pages, portrait (Letter or A4). Use one flame "path" that runs down both pages, and change its color as Montag changes.

**Color key (put it in a small legend at the bottom of page 1):**
- 🔴 Red / black = fire as **destruction** (the fireman)
- 🟠 Orange = fire as **rebellion / turning point**
- 🔵 Blue = water / **rebirth**
- 🟡 Gold / warm yellow = fire as **warmth, community, knowledge**

---

## PAGE 1

**Title (top banner):** FIRE REIMAGINED: How Bradbury Rewrites Montag's Flame

**Claim (box right under the title):**
> By the end of Part III, Montag changes from a man who destroys knowledge with fire into a man who preserves it. Bradbury shows this by changing what fire *means* to him: first a weapon, then a means of escape, and finally a source of warmth and rebirth.

**Theme statement (highlighted box):**
> A person's identity is defined less by the tools they use than by their purpose for using them. Real change means turning something destructive into something that gives life.

**Flame path, Stage 1: "It was a pleasure to burn" (Part I, a quick flashback)**
- Icon: fire helmet with "451"
- Color: black and red
- Text: *Before Part III, fire is Montag's identity. He burns without thinking.*

**Stage 2: Burning His Own House**
- Quote: "He burnt the bedroom walls and the cosmetics chest because he wanted to change everything" (Bradbury 113).
- Icon: house in flames
- Commentary: *Bradbury lets Montag hold the flamethrower one last time, but now he is burning away his own empty life. Fire starts to mean change instead of obedience.*

**Stage 3: Killing Beatty**
- Quote: "...all writhing flame on the lawn as Montag shot one continuous pulse of liquid fire on him" (Bradbury 119).
- Icon: cracked fire badge / broken salamander
- Color: orange-red
- Commentary: *The long, chaotic sentence matches the violence of the moment. Montag turns fire on authority itself, but the gruesome imagery shows that rebellion through destruction isn't the final answer.*

## PAGE 2

**Stage 4: The River**
- Quote: "He was moving from an unreality that was frightening into a reality that was unreal because it was new" (Bradbury 141).
- Icon: wavy water line that "puts out" the flame path
- Color: blue
- Commentary: *Water is the opposite of fire. Bradbury uses the river like a baptism: the fireman is washed away so that a new Montag can begin.*

**Stage 5: The Campfire**
- Quote: "It was not burning. It was warming" (Bradbury 145).
- Icon: campfire with people sitting around it
- Color: gold
- Commentary: *Two short, parallel sentences redefine the same symbol. Fire now brings people together instead of tearing things apart, and Montag sees himself as a preserver.*

**Stage 6: Rising From the Ashes (end of the novel)**
- Quote: "A time to break down, and a time to build up" (Bradbury 165).
- Icon: phoenix and an open book
- Commentary: *Granger's phoenix image completes the transformation. The city burns, but Montag carries Ecclesiastes in his memory, ready to help rebuild.*

**Small "Why Bradbury does this" strip:**
> Bradbury never removes fire from the story. He keeps the symbol and changes Montag's relationship to it, showing that a person changes from the inside, not by escaping their past.

## (GT Extension) Final Panel: "The Cost of Awakening"
Use a gray, ash-colored box with a burnt-paper edge.

> **What Montag loses:** his home, his marriage to Mildred, his career, his name and public identity (the government fakes his capture on TV), and his innocence when he kills Beatty. Even Faber and the city are lost when the bombs fall.
>
> **Why Bradbury makes the loss unavoidable:** In a society built on comfort and forgetting, nobody can wake up and stay safe. Bradbury ties awakening to sacrifice through the phoenix: something must burn completely before it can rise again. Montag's losses aren't a punishment. They are the price of becoming a real person, and they show that knowledge is worth more than comfort.
